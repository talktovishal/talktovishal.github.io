Learning by doing

The math prerequisites for understanding LLMs in-depth

In this chapter, we will build a tiny neural network, train it start to finish right here in your browser, and watch a pile of numbers learn to finish sentences.

Yes, there is math here. But every concept earns its keep by solving a problem you can actually see. Probability puts a number on how surprised the model is by the real next word. Vectors let it compare words. Loss squeezes a wrong guess into a single number. Gradients influence which way to nudge the numbers so the next guess is a little better. We will progressively build the math to gain a full understanding of how LLMs work under the hood.

We still keep the example deliberately tiny, because a tiny model lets you watch the whole loop. Chapter 3 showed what happens first: text is split into tokens, each token receives an integer ID, and that ID selects a row from the embedding table. Real tokenizers often split words into subword pieces. This browser model deliberately treats each whole word as one token so every number remains easy to inspect. From that simplified starting point, token IDs select embedding rows, those rows flow through the network to produce next-word probabilities, a loss is measured, and the parameters move a little closer to right. A modern LLM uses far more tokens and parameters, but the training beats are the same.

1. Surprise and the next word

Start where Chapter 1 left off: how surprised is the model?

One heads-up before we start. The other chapters keep the math light on purpose. This one does the opposite: we are going to write down the equations. Do not let that scare you off. Every symbol gets explained in plain words right where it appears, and every formula is just a tidy way of writing a concept you will have already worked out by hand a line above. Where a piece of calculus shows up, I will define it on the spot. Read the words first and the symbols second, and it will all make sense.

In Chapter 1, we learnt how to calculate perplexity from probability. To remind ourselves, perplexity is just a formulation of surprise. Now we will use it again to compare the evidence with a neural network's guesses.

Let's begin with a feeling you already have: surprise. Read "the cat ___" and your mind quietly bets on a couple of words - sat, maybe ran. End the sentence on sat and you feel nothing; end it on helicopter and you feel something is not right. A language model has that exact reflex, and all of training is just teaching the model to get less surprised and more confident at the words that really come next. So our very first job is to put a number on surprise.

To make this concrete, let's start with a small pile of next-word counts for a specific context. Say that after the phrase the cat, we look at our training data and see the word sat 8 times, ran 3 times, and slept once—12 examples in all. (These are round, hand-picked numbers so the math stays clean; the real corpus we actually train on at the end of the chapter is larger and messier, but the core idea is identical.) We can express the chance of sat coming next as P(sat | the cat) = 8 / 12 ≈ 0.67 (or 67%). The other two probabilities come from the same count-over-total calculation: P(ran | the cat) = 3 / 12 = 0.25 (25%), and P(slept | the cat) = 1 / 12 ≈ 0.083 (about 8%; the panel rounds to whole percentages). The exact fractions add up to 12 / 12 = 1, or 100%. That vertical bar means "given": we are not asking how common the word sat is everywhere, only how common it is right after the cat. This represents the true distribution of our data.

When training a language model, we want its predictions to match this reality as closely as possible. To measure how well it's doing, we rely on three heavily connected concepts from Information Theory. All three answer one fundamental question: is the model confident, or flailing? Let's look at them through the lens of "surprise."

To keep the two sides straight, we will call the true distribution in the evidence p. In the same order as the words above - sat, ran, then slept - p = (8/12, 3/12, 1/12) ≈ (0.67, 0.25, 0.083). This answers, "What actually happened in the evidence?" We will call the model's current guess q = (0.55, 0.30, 0.15). This answers, "What does the model currently believe?" The two distributions cover the same three words, but they are supposed to differ before training: p is the target and q is the imperfect guess. Entropy asks how much uncertainty remains even if we know p perfectly. Cross-entropy asks how surprised the model actually is while it uses q. Training nudges q toward p; when they match, cross-entropy reaches the entropy floor.

Entropy is the inherent uncertainty of the language itself. Imagine you knew the exact true probabilities (like our 67%, 25%, and 8%). Even with perfect knowledge, you still can't be 100% sure what the next word will be; there is a baseline level of average surprise when the next word is finally revealed. Entropy calculates this absolute floor, measured in bits. It is the smallest average surprise anyone could manage on this context. If a context forces exactly one specific next word every single time, the entropy is zero bits—no surprise at all.

Cross-entropy, on the other hand, is the model's actual average surprise when it uses its own internal, imperfect probabilities to predict the real next words. If our model incorrectly thinks slept is the most likely next word, it will incur a massive "surprise penalty" every time sat repeatedly shows up in the training data. The closer the model's predictions match the true distribution, the lower the cross-entropy gets, eventually bottoming out exactly at the true Entropy floor. In machine learning, minimizing cross-entropy is the standard way we teach models to predict text.

This formula was not chosen only because the intuition sounds right. A useful information measure must assign zero surprise to certainty, assign more surprise to less likely events, and add the surprises of independent events even though their probabilities multiply. Under mild regularity assumptions, those requirements force a negative logarithm. Averaging that surprise over what reality actually produces gives \(-\sum p\log q\). The optional deep dive below derives this carefully and shows why coding theory, honest probability forecasts, and maximum-likelihood training all arrive at the same expression.

As you saw in Chapter 1, perplexity rewrites this cross-entropy metric on a much friendlier, intuitive scale. While "bits of surprise" can feel abstract, perplexity translates those bits back into an effective vocabulary size. Mathematically, it is simply the exponentiation of cross-entropy (e.g., 2cross-entropy). If a model has a perplexity of 4 on a given text, it means that, on average, the model felt as confused as if it were choosing blindly between 4 equally likely words. A perfect model that knows exactly what comes next has a perplexity of 1. In this chapter, it stays beside cross-entropy as a readable translation of the same surprise, not as a separate training objective.

Now feel it for yourself. The left sliders are the evidence - how often each word actually followed the context. The right sliders are the model's guesses. Drag a guess away from the evidence and watch cross-entropy climb; drag it back and watch the surprise fall toward the entropy floor. That falling number is exactly what training will do for us, automatically, thousands of times over.

The same idea, in symbols

Everything we just discussed has a compact mathematical form. The core building block is the surprise of a single word. If our model assigns a probability \(q\) to a specific word, the surprise of seeing that word is measured as \(-\log_2(q)\) bits. If our model assigns probability \(q\) to a word, its surprise is \(-\log_2(q)\) bits of ideal information. That value can be fractional; it is not claiming that one real codeword or one game of 20 Questions literally uses a fractional question. Notice how this aligns with our intuition: if the model is highly confident (\(q\) is close to 1), the surprise drops to nearly zero. But if the model thinks a word is almost impossible (\(q\) is close to 0), the resulting surprise penalty is massive.

To evaluate the model's overall performance, we don't just look at one word; we calculate the expected (or average) surprise. We take the surprise of every possible next word and weight it by how often that word truly appears in the evidence (\(p\)). Adding all these weighted surprises together gives us the Entropy (if using the true probabilities \(p\) for the surprise) or Cross-Entropy (if using the model's imperfect guesses \(q\) for the surprise):

$$H(p) = -\sum_{w} p(w)\,\log_2 p(w) \qquad H(p,q) = -\sum_{w} p(w)\,\log_2 q(w)$$ $$\text{Perplexity} = 2^{H(p,q)}$$
  • \(p(w)\): The true distribution — how often word \(w\) actually follows this context in the data.
  • \(q(w)\): The model's prediction — the probability the model assigns to word \(w\).
  • \(\sum_{w}\): The sum over the entire vocabulary. We add up the probability-weighted surprise for every candidate next word.
  • \(\log_2\): Measures the surprise in bits, framing the prediction as binary choices. Perplexity turns those abstract bits back into an intuitive effective choice count; perplexity itself has no unit.

Go deeper: why cross-entropy has this exact form

The intuition is useful, but the formula was not an inspired guess. Several different lines of reasoning constrain us toward the same expression. We will start with the requirements for one event's surprise, then average that surprise over reality, and finally check the result against coding theory and model training.

1. The requirements force a logarithm

Call the surprise of an event with probability \(q\) by the name \(I(q)\). A sensible information measure should obey a few rules:

  • Certainty means zero surprise: \(I(1)=0\).
  • Rarer events are more surprising: as \(q\) gets smaller, \(I(q)\) gets larger.
  • Small probability changes cause small surprise changes: \(I(q)\) should be continuous rather than jumping unpredictably.
  • Independent surprises add: if independent events have probabilities \(q_1\) and \(q_2\), their joint probability is \(q_1q_2\), while their information should add.
$$I(q_1q_2)=I(q_1)+I(q_2)$$

Continuous logarithms are exactly the functions that turn multiplication into addition. Those requirements therefore give \(I(q)=-K\log q\), where \(K>0\) chooses the unit. The minus sign makes the surprise non-negative because the logarithm of a probability below 1 is negative.

  • Choose base 2 and \(K=1\): surprise is measured in bits.
  • Choose the natural log and \(K=1\): surprise is measured in nats.

That is why each halving of probability adds one bit: \(-\log_2(1/2)=1\), \(-\log_2(1/4)=2\), and \(-\log_2(1/8)=3\).

2. Reality supplies the weights; the model supplies the surprise

Now suppose reality produces outcome \(i\) with probability \(p_i\), while the model assigns that outcome probability \(q_i\). Whenever outcome \(i\) occurs, the model pays \(-\log q_i\) surprise. Reality makes some outcomes occur more often than others, so the average surprise is the expectation under \(p\):

$$H(p,q)=\mathbb{E}_{i\sim p}[-\log q_i]= -\sum_i p_i\log q_i$$
  • \(p_i\) says how often reality makes us pay for row \(i\).
  • \(q_i\) determines how expensive or surprising that row is when it occurs.

This is the same division of labor in the worked example above: evidence \(p\) supplies the frequency weights, and model belief \(q\) supplies each word's surprise. That expected surprise is cross-entropy.

3. The same formula is expected coding cost

Now look past the metaphor of surprise. In an ideal code based on model probability \(q_i\), outcome \(i\) has information length \(-\log_2 q_i\) bits. Frequent model outcomes get short descriptions; outcomes the model considers rare get long descriptions.

Imagine playing 20 Questions to identify the next word, where every yes/no question costs one bit. Probabilities that are exact powers of two make the relationship especially visible:

  • A 50% event needs \(-\log_2(0.5)=1\) bit.
  • A 25% event needs \(-\log_2(0.25)=2\) bits.
  • A 12.5% event needs \(-\log_2(0.125)=3\) bits.

Real prefix codes use whole-bit codewords, so an individual codeword is not always exactly \(-\log_2 q_i\) bits. The source coding theorem says that, over long sequences, good codes can approach this ideal average. If the data really follows \(p\) but the code was designed from \(q\), its expected ideal length is \(-\sum_i p_i\log_2q_i\): cross-entropy in bits.

So a bad model wastes bits. If sat occurs often under \(p\) but the model gives it \(q=0.01\), we repeatedly pay about \(6.64\) bits for a common event.

4. Why reporting the true probabilities wins

Cross-entropy separates into the unavoidable uncertainty in reality and an extra penalty for using the wrong model:

$$H(p,q)=H(p)+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)$$ $$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)=\sum_i p_i\log\frac{p_i}{q_i}\ge 0$$

The KL divergence cannot be negative, and it is zero when \(q=p\) (apart from zero-probability edge cases). Therefore \(H(p,q)\ge H(p)\): the model cannot beat reality's own entropy floor, and cross-entropy reaches that floor when its probabilities match reality.

In forecasting language, negative log probability is a strictly proper scoring rule: in expectation, the best strategy is to report your real probabilities rather than pretending to be more or less confident. That makes cross-entropy useful for learning honest probability distributions, not merely choosing the winning class.

5. Maximum likelihood arrives at the same objective

Suppose the training data contains observed outcomes \(x_1,\ldots,x_N\), and a model with parameters \(\theta\) assigns each one probability \(q_\theta(x_n)\). Maximum likelihood asks for parameters that make the entire observed dataset as probable as possible:

$$\max_\theta\prod_{n=1}^{N}q_\theta(x_n)$$

Logs turn the product into a sum. Negating and dividing by \(N\) turns maximization into minimization:

$$\min_\theta -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\ln q_\theta(x_n)$$

That is empirical cross-entropy: the average negative log probability assigned to the outcomes that actually occurred. With one-hot targets, each example contributes only \(-\ln q_{\text{true}}\), exactly the loss used later in the Softmax section.

6. The historical thread

Ralph Hartley used logarithms in 1928 to measure information through the number of possible messages. In 1948, Claude Shannon gave the modern communication-theory treatment: he formalized entropy's defining properties and connected it to the limits of coding. Cross-entropy is the natural next case: reality follows \(p\), but our code or model uses \(q\).

These viewpoints meet at one formula for a reason. The logarithm satisfies the information rules; the expectation under \(p\) measures real average cost; KL divergence guarantees truth is optimal; and maximum likelihood turns observed data into the same training loss.

2. Words as vectors

Representing words as vectors - a list of numbers.

EntrophyEntropy tells us how wrong our guess was. But before the model can guess at all, it needs a way to tell words apart - and, more usefully, to notice when two words are alike. You already do this without thinking: cat and dog feel like cousins, while cat and table feel like strangers. We want to give the model that same gut feeling, using nothing but numbers. Chapter 2's answer was to hand each word a short list of learned numbers, its vector. Stack all those rows into one table and you have an embedding matrix: one row per word, and "looking up a word" is simply grabbing its row. The row is not a dictionary definition; it is something that the learning algorithm can tune. Here we shrink each word down to just two numbers, so we can draw it as an arrow on a grid and actually see what the model sees.

Why arrows? Because we can compare two word vectors with one humble operation: the dot product. Multiply the matching numbers of two vectors, add the results, done. If that feels too small to power a language model, good - hold on to the surprise. The payoff is geometric: that single number runs large when two arrows point the same way, near zero when they are unrelated, and negative when they point in opposite directions. Cosine similarity is the same idea stripped down to direction alone, and distance just asks how far apart the tips sit. Cosine tells us whether these two embedding rows point alike. In the next section, we will reuse the dot product inside the network itself.

A quick map of number arrangements

One number, one row, one table, one batch.

Before we use these words in formulas, let us attach each one to something already on the page. Each object below is a different number arrangement. We describe that arrangement by counting along each axis: how many rows, how many numbers per row, and, when we stack several tables, how many tables. Code libraries call that list of counts the array's shape. So 4 x 2 means four rows with two numbers in each row.

  • Scalar: one number on its own, such as the cross-entropy loss 1.24. It tells us how wrong one result is.
  • Vector: one ordered row of numbers, such as the cat embedding [0.70, -0.30]. Its number arrangement is one axis of length 2 because this teaching vector holds two numbers.
  • Matrix: vectors stacked into a rectangular table. The four embedding rows for cat, dog, mat, and rug form a 4 x 2 matrix: four rows, two numbers per row. The network's layer weights are matrices too.

Why do we need a tensor?

A model rarely processes only one training example at a time. Suppose we send it these two contexts together: the cat and the dog. Each word already has a two-number embedding:

example 1, "the cat" = [[0.20, 0.10], [0.70, -0.30]] example 2, "the dog" = [[0.20, 0.10], [0.60, -0.20]] batch = [ example 1, example 2 ]

Each example is a 2 x 2 matrix: two context words, with two embedding numbers for each word. Stacking the two matrices gives the computer one object with three axes. Machine-learning libraries call that object a tensor. Its code-library shape is 2 x 2 x 2, and now each 2 has a concrete job:

  1. First axis - 2 examples: should I read the cat or the dog?
  2. Second axis - 2 context positions: should I read the first word or the second word in that example?
  3. Third axis - 2 embedding numbers: which coordinate of that word's vector should I read?

That is the motivation for a tensor: it keeps several related tables in one organized object without losing which example, word position, or vector coordinate each number belongs to. Tensor is also the umbrella term used by machine-learning libraries: a scalar is a zero-axis tensor, a vector is a one-axis tensor, a matrix is a two-axis tensor, and this batch is a three-axis tensor.

No new calculation is hiding here. These names only tell us how the numbers are arranged. In this section we inspect one vector and its matrix; later, the same number-arrangement language, and the code term shape, will help us read the network's weight tables.

The same comparison, in symbols

Why does multiplying and adding numbers measure meaning? Fort nowFor now, imagine a coordinate trying to measure a hidden trait such as "furriness" or "royalty." Chapter 2's important caveat still applies: real dimensions are not individually labelled features; the useful pattern is distributed across the whole vector. This is just for us to get an intuition. When you multiply matching components, you are checking for shared coordinates. If both cat and dog score high on "furry," multiplying those values produces a large number. Adding these up across all dimensions yields the dot product—a raw score of alignment in this intuition.

This mathematical trick wasn't invented for language. In the 1880s, physicists like Josiah Willard Gibbs formulated vector algebra to calculate how much of one physical force aligns with another (like wind pushing against a sail). They proved that the dot product inherently captures the geometric angle (\(\theta\)) between two arrows.

For language models, this geometric angle is crucial. Raw dot products have a flaw: they grow larger simply if a vector is longer (e.g., if a word appears more frequently during training). By dividing the dot product by the lengths of the two vectors, we strip away the magnitude. We are left only with the direction they point. This is cosine similarity:

$$a \cdot b = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i b_i \qquad \lVert a \rVert = \sqrt{a \cdot a} \qquad \cos\theta = \frac{a \cdot b}{\lVert a \rVert\,\lVert b \rVert}$$
  • \(a_i\): the \(i\)-th coordinate inside vector \(a\); one coordinate has no reliable human label on its own.
  • \(n\): how many numbers each vector holds (just 2 in this demo, but thousands in an LLM).
  • \(\lVert a \rVert\): the magnitude or overall length of the arrow \(a\).
  • \(\theta\): the angle between the two arrows. If they point the exact same way (identical meaning), the angle is 0°, and \(\cos(0) = 1\).

With \(\text{cat} = [0.7, -0.3]\) and \(\text{dog} = [0.6, -0.2]\): \(a \cdot b = 0.48\), \(\lVert \text{cat} \rVert \approx 0.762\), \(\lVert \text{dog} \rVert \approx 0.632\), so \(\cos\theta \approx 0.997\). This cosine tells us that the two embedding rows point in nearly the same direction. That is all we are measuring here. 3. Build the forward pass

Stack the pieces into a tiny network we can inspect.

We have three ingredients so far - probability, word vectors, and the dot product. In this section, we will assemble them into a neural network to predict the next word. We will produce a raw score for every possible next word. In the next section we will convert the scores into softmax by turning them into probabilities. Think of a neural network as a pipeline. The input data (our context words) is the water flowing through the pipes, and the weights are the adjustable valves. The water always flows forward, but the valves decide how much pressure makes it to each possible next-word output.

The smallest valve intersection is a neuron: it multiplies its inputs by weights, adds a bias, and runs the result through an activation function. Picture it as a tiny opinion-maker: it listens to a few numbers, weighs each one by how much it trusts that input, adds its own standing belief (the bias), and then decides how strongly to speak up. Why bother with the activation? Because without that little bend - a ReLU, a \(\tanh\), a sigmoid - a stack of layers can only ever draw a straight line, no matter how tall you build it. The bend or non-linearity is what lets it curve and learn complex patterns. To understand this, consider: stacking two plain multiply-and-add steps just gives you another multiply-and-add. Push x through one layer to get w1x + b1, feed that into a second, and you land on w2(w1x + b1) + b2 - which tidies up into (w2w1)x + (w2b1 + b2), a single weight and a single bias all over again. A hundred such layers collapse back into one straight line. The activation's non-linearity is exactly what breaks that collapse: once each layer can curve, stacking them lets the network trace shapes a straight line never could. Line up several neurons and you have a layer; stack the layers and you have a multilayer perceptron, or MLP.

This is the part of the forward pass we will build in this section: look up an embedding row for each context (adjoining) word, join the rows, push them through a hidden layer with an activation, and produce one logit, or raw pre-probability score, for each candidate word. 4. Softmax and cross-entropy

Turn raw scores into probabilities, then calculate cross-entropy loss.

Once we run the network to get its output, it will not naturally hand us neat percentages for the list of possible words. It will hand us a raw score for every word it could predict - a number called a logit. A logit can be 4.2 or -1.1 or anything at all, and that is perfectly legal. The problem is not the minus sign; logits are raw relative scores, not probabilities. They do not have to be positive or add up to 1. If all we wanted was the single winning word, the logits would be enough: choose the largest one. But a raw logit such as 4.2 does not mean 42% confident, so logits do not tell us what share of the model's belief belongs to each candidate. Probabilities make every word share one fixed budget of 1, or 100%. If sat receives 0.68, exactly 0.32 remains for all the other words. That shared scale lets us sample plausible alternatives and gives cross-entropy the probability assigned to the word that actually occurred.

What this section finishes

Two jobs: create a probability distribution, then calculate cross-entropy loss.

1Softmax creates probabilities

It turns the three logits into probabilities \(q\) that are positive and add to 1.

2Cross-entropy calculates loss

It uses the probability assigned to the word that actually came next to calculate one loss \(L\).

Softmax is the elegant bridge that gets us from any-old-numbers to a clean distribution. It relies on a two-step trick: first, it exponentiates every score. This instantly turns every number (even negative ones) positive, and it stretches the gaps between close numbers so a clear winner emerges. Second, it divides each by the running total so the whole set perfectly sums to 1. Temperature is the dial that controls that stretching. Chapter 1 gave you a coarse version of this same choice - top-pick (always take the strongest word) versus sampling (weighted random). Why does one dial do both? Because temperature acts before the exponential: every score is divided by T first. Make T large and you squash all the scores toward zero, so after exponentiating they land near e0 = 1 - nearly equal, a flat tie where even long-shot words get a real chance. Make T small and you stretch the gaps between scores wide open, so the exponential of the biggest one towers over the rest and runs away with almost all the probability. Push T all the way down toward zero and the single largest score wins outright - which is exactly Chapter 1's top-pick, now recovered as one end of a continuous dial.

Once Softmax gives us probabilities, we calculate the cross-entropy loss. It asks one focused question: how much probability did the model give the word that actually came next? High probability for the true word means low surprise and low loss; tiny probability means high surprise and high loss.

Earlier, in Cross-entropy by hand, we averaged surprise over the evidence distribution \(p\): words that occurred more often received more weight. Training presents the same idea one observed example at a time. In this example, exactly one word actually came next: sat. We record that fact with the target vector \(y=[1,0,0]\), in the same sat, ran, slept order as the probabilities \(q\).

The earlier worked example used \(\log_2\), so its surprise was measured in bits. Training code conventionally uses the natural log \(\ln\), so this loss is measured in nats. The reasoning is identical; only the unit changes. Start from the full one-example cross-entropy sum, then substitute that target vector:

\(L=-\sum_i y_i\ln q_i\) \(L=-\big(1\ln q_{\text{sat}}+0\ln q_{\text{ran}}+0\ln q_{\text{slept}}\big)\) \(L=-\ln q_{\text{sat}}=-\ln q_{\text{true}}\)

The two zero-weighted terms disappear, leaving only the surprise of the word that actually occurred. Averaging this one-example loss across the training data recovers the distribution-level cross-entropy from the earlier worked example: frequent outcomes contribute the same term more often.

With the default probabilities below, the true word sat receives \(q_{\text{sat}}\approx0.681\). The live calculation keeps the unrounded probability \(0.681487\), so its loss is \(-\ln(0.681487)\approx0.383\) nats. If the model gave the true word only \(0.10\), the loss would jump to \(-\ln(0.10)\approx2.303\) nats. Cross-entropy therefore gives the model one scalar score that clearly distinguishes a confident correct guess from a poor one.

This section completes both the probability distribution \(q\) and the loss \(L\). The next section introduces gradients. Backpropagation later differentiates this loss with respect to each logit and carries those derivatives backward; it does not introduce or finish cross-entropy itself.

Stable Softmax and cross-entropy, in symbols

These equations are just the four walkthrough steps above written as shorthand; nothing new is being introduced. Start with original logit \(z_i\) for candidate \(i\). Temperature turns it into scaled score \(s_i=z_i/T\): a lower \(T\) spreads the scores apart, a higher \(T\) pulls them together, and their ranking stays unchanged because every logit is divided by the same positive number.

$$s_i=\frac{z_i}{T} \qquad m=\max_j s_j$$

Next choose \(m\), the largest scaled score, and subtract it from every score. The largest shifted score becomes 0 and all the others become non-positive, so no exponential can grow beyond \(e^0=1\). This is the numerically stable form software uses.

$$q_i=\frac{e^{s_i-m}}{\sum_j e^{s_j-m}}$$

Why is that subtraction allowed? It multiplies every exponential by the same factor \(e^{-m}\). The factor appears in both numerator and denominator and cancels, so the probabilities are exactly the same as ordinary Softmax:

$$\frac{e^{s_i-m}}{\sum_j e^{s_j-m}} =\frac{e^{-m}e^{s_i}}{e^{-m}\sum_j e^{s_j}} =\frac{e^{s_i}}{\sum_j e^{s_j}}$$

Exponentiation makes every amount positive. Dividing by their shared total gives probabilities \(q_i\) that sum to 1 while preserving the score ranking. Softmax has now completed its job: the raw scores have become one model distribution \(q\).

Cross-entropy now calculates the loss from that distribution. The target vector \(y\) has a 1 at the word that actually came next and 0 everywhere else, so only the true word's term survives. This is the one-example version of the distribution-level cross-entropy from the opening section:

$$L=-\sum_i y_i\ln q_i=-\ln q_{\text{true}}$$
  • \(i\): the candidate we are currently talking about; \(j\): the index used to scan all candidates in a maximum or sum.
  • \(z_i\): the original raw logit for candidate \(i\); it can be any real number.
  • \(T\): temperature; \(T=1\) leaves logits unchanged, lower sharpens, and higher flattens.
  • \(s_i=z_i/T\): candidate \(i\)'s temperature-scaled score.
  • \(m\): the largest scaled score, subtracted from every score only for numerical safety.
  • \(q_i\): the resulting probability for candidate \(i\); every \(q_i\) is positive and \(\sum_i q_i=1\).
  • \(y_i\): the target for candidate \(i\), 1 for the word that actually came next and 0 for every other word.
  • \(L\): the completed cross-entropy loss in nats, the negative natural log of the true word's probability.

Read the final line in plain words: look up how much probability the model gave the true word, take its negative natural log, and call that one scalar \(L\). High true-word probability gives low loss; low true-word probability gives high loss. No trainable parameter changes during these equations. This is still the forward calculation.

5. Gradients, gradient descent, and loss

When the guess is wrong, which way do we nudge?

We can now measure surprise and compare words - but measuring a mistake is not the same as fixing it. Here is the question that actually does the learning: when a guess is wrong, which way, and how far, should we move the numbers? Let's start simple, we will shrink the model to the simplest thing that can possibly learn - a straight line with two knobs, a slope and an intercept - and ask it to pass through a cloud of dots. The mean squared error (MSE) loss is just the average squared gap between the line and the dots: one number that answers "how wrong are we right now?". (Our word model will later swap in cross-entropy loss, but the nudging idea is exactly the same.) MSE and cross-entropy are different objectives on different scales, so their raw loss numbers should not be compared with each other.

Loss, gradient, and the update rule, in symbols

The loss is the average squared gap between the line's prediction \(\hat{y}\) and the true value \(y\). To fix a bad prediction, we need to know how tweaking our knobs affects that loss. In machine learning, we group all adjustable knobs (like our slope and our intercept) under the universal label of weights, represented by the letter \(w\). Think of \(w\) as a generic blank space: the update rule at the bottom is a template. You run the template once substituting \(w\) for the slope, and then you run it again substituting \(w\) for the intercept.

The gradient \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}\) reads as "how fast does the loss \(L\) change if I nudge just this one specific weight \(w\), holding the rest perfectly still." That is all the partial derivative \(\partial\) symbol means: isolating one knob at a time.

Calculus Refreshers: If the \(\partial\) symbol looks unfamiliar, Wikipedia has excellent, visual primers on Partial derivatives and the Chain Rule.

How do we calculate that exact gradient number? We use the Chain Rule. For one data point, the \(2\) from the squared error drops down to the front, and then we multiply by the inner variable attached to our knob. Because our prediction is \((\text{slope} \cdot x + \text{intercept})\), the variable attached to the slope is \(x\). The variable attached to the intercept is just \(1\). Those are the per-point contributions. Because the loss above averages over \(N\) points, the full gradient adds all \(N\) contributions and divides by \(N\) too.

$$L = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(\hat{y}_i - y_i\big)^2 \qquad (\text{where } \hat{y} = \text{slope} \cdot x + \text{intercept})$$ $$\frac{\partial L}{\partial\,\text{slope}} = \frac{2}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{y}_i-y_i)x_i \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial\,\text{intercept}} = \frac{2}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{y}_i-y_i)$$ $$w \leftarrow w - \eta\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}$$
  • \(\hat{y}\): the line's prediction; \(y\): the true value in the data.
  • \(N\): how many points we average the loss over.
  • \(w\): a generic "weight"—a placeholder. You swap in either "slope" or "intercept" when applying the math.
  • \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}\): the gradient, representing the uphill slope of the loss for that specific weight \(w\).
  • \(\eta\) (eta): the learning rate or step size (the same slider used in the lab).
  • \(\leftarrow\): "becomes" (we replace the old weight with its newly nudged value).

Two derivatives here become millions of derivatives later

This line has two trainable knobs, so its gradient is the two-number vector below. A neural network follows the identical rule; its gradient vector simply has one entry for every embedding value, weight, and bias.

∇Lline = [∂L/∂slope, ∂L/∂intercept] ∇Lnetwork = [∂L/∂E1,1, …, ∂L/∂W2,1,1, …, ∂L/∂b2,V]

Backpropagation's job is to calculate that longer vector efficiently. Gradient descent's job stays separate: it subtracts the completed vector from the parameters.

Walking through one step for the slope: This is the \(N=1\) special case, so the average contains one term and \(1/N=1\). For a single data point \((x{=}1,\ y{=}1)\) and a starting \(\text{slope}{=}0\), our prediction is \(\hat{y}=0\). The error is \(\hat{y}-y = -1\).

The gradient for the slope is \(2(-1)(1) = -2\). Now we use the update rule template, swapping \(w\) for the slope: \(\text{slope} \leftarrow 0 - 0.1(-2) = +0.2\). Subtracting the gradient ensures we step downhill, and \(\eta\) (set to \(0.1\) here) scales how big that step is.

6. Backpropagation

Flow the blame backward to fix the weights.

We know the loss, and the preceding gradients section showed what a gradient means. But our tiny network has many knobs buried across two layers. Imagine a factory assembly line: the boss at the end yells, "This car is ruined!" The final assembler replies, "The chassis arrived crooked from the previous station." To fix the factory, we pass responsibility backward through every station in proportion to how strongly that station affected the result.

That backward accounting is backpropagation. It uses the chain rule to calculate one gradient for every trainable parameter. It does not update a single parameter. Only after backprop has finished does gradient descent apply those gradients with \(\theta \leftarrow \theta-\eta\nabla\theta\).

The output error that starts the backward pass

The Softmax section scaled each logit with \(s_i=z_i/T\). Softmax plus cross-entropy gives \(\partial L/\partial s_i=q_i-y_i\). The chain rule through \(s_i=z_i/T\) adds \(1/T\):

$$\delta_i=\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i}=\frac{q_i-y_i}{T}$$

The worksheet uses \(T=1\), so its output blame is the familiar \(\delta=q-y\). Nothing has been dropped; the temperature factor simply equals 1 here.

Why does Softmax plus cross-entropy simplify to \(q-y\)?

This is the detailed cancellation that used to appear in the Softmax section. It belongs here because it calculates a derivative. Work first with the temperature-scaled score \(s_i=z_i/T\). The stable maximum subtraction from the forward pass does not change \(q\), so we can write ordinary Softmax compactly as \(q_i=e^{s_i}/\sum_j e^{s_j}\).

Let \(c\) be the index of the true word. Cross-entropy for this one example is \(L=-\ln q_c\). Substitute the Softmax definition, then use \(\ln(a/b)=\ln a-\ln b\):

$$L=-\ln\frac{e^{s_c}}{\sum_j e^{s_j}} =-s_c+\ln\sum_j e^{s_j}$$

Now ask how \(L\) changes when one scaled score \(s_i\) moves. The two terms make different contributions:

  • \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial s_i}(-s_c)=-y_i\): this term touches only the true word. It contributes \(-1\) when \(i=c\) and 0 for every other candidate.
  • \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial s_i}\ln\sum_j e^{s_j} =\dfrac{e^{s_i}}{\sum_j e^{s_j}}=q_i\): the derivative of the shared log-sum is the Softmax probability itself.

Add those two contributions:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial s_i}=q_i-y_i$$

Finally carry that derivative through the temperature operation \(s_i=z_i/T\). Its local derivative is \(1/T\):

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i} =\frac{\partial L}{\partial s_i}\frac{\partial s_i}{\partial z_i} =(q_i-y_i)\frac{1}{T}$$

In plain words: raise the true word's logit and lower every other logit in proportion to the probability it currently holds. The correction is self-scaling. For the true word it is \(q_c-1\): small when the model is already confident, and close to \(-1\) when it gave the truth almost no probability. For other words it is \(q_i\), so an incorrect word that is hogging probability receives the largest downward correction.

As \(q\) approaches the one-hot target \(y\), every \(q_i-y_i\) approaches 0 and the output gradient vanishes. The worksheet's backward half begins by calculating exactly this \(T=1\) error vector.

Math refresher

The tiny calculus toolkit for the backward half.

When you reach Step 9, these are the local rules we use. You can read them as a cheat sheet while the worksheet asks you to do the actual arithmetic.

Partial derivative
\(\partial L / \partial W_{\text{sat},h1}\) means: nudge just this one weight, hold the rest still, and ask how fast the loss changes.
Show why this notation means one knob

An ordinary derivative watches one input move. A partial derivative does the same thing when the function has many inputs: move one input and freeze the others.

\(L = L(W_{\text{sat},h1}, W_{\text{sat},h2}, \ldots)\) \(L_{\text{after}} = L(W_{\text{sat},h1}+\epsilon,\text{others fixed})\) \(L_{\text{before}} = L(W_{\text{sat},h1},\text{others fixed})\) \(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial W_{\text{sat},h1}} = \lim_{\epsilon \to 0}\dfrac{L_{\text{after}} - L_{\text{before}}}{\epsilon}\)

So the symbol is not new magic. It is a tiny "what if I turn only this knob?" experiment.

Chain rule
If \(W\) changes \(z\), and \(z\) changes \(L\), multiply the two local slopes: \(\partial L/\partial W = (\partial L/\partial z)(\partial z/\partial W)\).
Show why slopes multiply

Imagine nudging \(W\) by a tiny amount \(\Delta W\). First that nudge changes \(z\). Then the change in \(z\) changes the loss.

\(\Delta z \approx \dfrac{\partial z}{\partial W}\Delta W\) \(\Delta L \approx \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial z}\Delta z\) \(\Delta L \approx \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial z}\left(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial W}\Delta W\right)\) \(\dfrac{\Delta L}{\Delta W} \approx \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial z}\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial W}\)

As the nudge gets tiny, the approximation becomes the derivative. That is the chain rule.

Many paths add
If one hidden neuron feeds several word scores, add each path's contribution: one blame term times one outgoing weight per word.
Show why paths add

A hidden value \(h\) can affect the loss through many scores at once. The total loss change is the sum of the loss change coming through each score.

\(\Delta L \approx \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial z_1}\Delta z_1 + \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial z_2}\Delta z_2 + \cdots\) \(\Delta z_i \approx \dfrac{\partial z_i}{\partial h}\Delta h\) \(\Delta L \approx \left(\sum_i \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial z_i}\dfrac{\partial z_i}{\partial h}\right)\Delta h\) \(\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial h} = \sum_i \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial z_i}\dfrac{\partial z_i}{\partial h}\)

Backprop is doing careful bookkeeping: every downstream route gets its say, then we add them.

Natural log
Here \(\log\) means natural log. The key rule is \(d(\ln q)/dq = 1/q\), so \(d(-\ln q)/dq = -1/q\).
Show why the log slope is 1/q

The natural log is the inverse of the exponential. If \(y = \ln q\), then exponentiating both sides gives \(e^y = q\).

\(y = \ln q\) \(e^y = q\) \(\dfrac{d}{dq}(e^y) = \dfrac{d}{dq}(q)\) \(e^y\dfrac{dy}{dq} = 1\) \(\dfrac{dy}{dq} = \dfrac{1}{e^y}\) \(\dfrac{dy}{dq} = \dfrac{1}{q}\)

So \(\ln q\) gets flatter as \(q\) gets larger. The minus sign in \(-\ln q\) just flips the slope: \(-1/q\).

Exp and softmax
\(d(e^z)/dz = e^z\). Softmax uses those exponentials, and its shared denominator is why one score affects every probability.
Show why exp and softmax behave this way

The exponential is special because a tiny increase keeps the same shape scaled by the current value.

\(\dfrac{d(e^z)}{dz} = \lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\dfrac{e^{z+\epsilon}-e^z}{\epsilon}\) \(= e^z\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\dfrac{e^\epsilon-1}{\epsilon}\) \(= e^z\)

That last limit equals 1 for the natural base \(e\). Softmax then divides one exponential by the shared total \(S\).

\(q_i = e^{z_i}/S,\quad S=\sum_k e^{z_k}\) \(\dfrac{\partial q_i}{\partial z_i}=q_i(1-q_i)\) \(\dfrac{\partial q_j}{\partial z_i}=-q_jq_i\quad\text{when }j\ne i\)

One score raising its own numerator also raises the denominator, so other probabilities get squeezed down.

Linear weights
For a score like \(z = Wh + b\), the slope with respect to \(W\) is the signal \(h\), and the slope with respect to \(h\) is the weight \(W\).
Show why the signal and weight appear

Hold \(h\) and \(b\) fixed, then nudge \(W\) by a tiny amount \(\epsilon\).

\(z(W+\epsilon) = (W+\epsilon)h+b\) \(z(W+\epsilon)-z(W)=\epsilon h\) \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial W}=\lim_{\epsilon\to0}\dfrac{\epsilon h}{\epsilon}=h\)

Now hold \(W\) and \(b\) fixed, then nudge \(h\).

\(z(h+\epsilon)=W(h+\epsilon)+b\) \(z(h+\epsilon)-z(h)=W\epsilon\) \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial h}=W\)
ReLU slope
ReLU passes positive raw values with slope 1 and blocks negative raw values with slope 0.
Show why the gate is 1 or 0

ReLU is a piecewise function. It is one straight line on the positive side and a flat line on the negative side.

\(\operatorname{ReLU}(r)=\max(0,r)\) \(\operatorname{ReLU}(r)=r\quad\text{if }r>0\) \(\operatorname{ReLU}(r)=0\quad\text{if }r<0\)

The derivative of \(r\) is 1. The derivative of a flat constant 0 is 0.

\(\operatorname{ReLU}'(r)=1\quad\text{if }r>0\) \(\operatorname{ReLU}'(r)=0\quad\text{if }r<0\)

At exactly \(r=0\), the sharp corner has no single slope. In practice, code chooses a convention; here we treat it as 0.

Gradient step
The gradient points uphill, toward higher loss. To lower the loss, gradient descent subtracts it: \(W_{\text{new}} = W_{\text{old}} - \eta g\).
Show why subtracting moves downhill

For a tiny move \(\Delta W\), the derivative gives a local estimate of the new loss.

\(g = \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial W}\) \(L(W+\Delta W) \approx L(W)+g\Delta W\)

If we choose the move \(\Delta W=-\eta g\), where \(\eta\) is a small positive learning rate, the predicted loss change becomes negative.

\(L(W-\eta g)\approx L(W)+g(-\eta g)\) \(L(W-\eta g)\approx L(W)-\eta g^2\)

Because \(g^2\) cannot be negative, subtracting the gradient aims the step downhill.

One small notation note: \(\mathbb{1}_{i=j}\) is just an on/off switch. It equals 1 when the two indexes match and 0 otherwise.

A Step-by-Step Backpropagation Walkthrough

To truly demystify backpropagation, we are going to start by ditching the abstract algebra and crunching real numbers step-by-step (an approach heavily inspired by Matt Mazur's classic backpropagation tutorial, but adapted for our language model). Once we prove the math works for a single weight, we will scale it up using matrices—revealing why operations like "transpose" are the secret weapons that make modern AI possible.

We will trace exactly how a mistake at the end of the network flows backward to fix a weight at the very beginning.

To see this sweep in action, we need to unwind the forward pass we just built. Here is our network's forward logic, read from bottom to top:

$$x = [\,E_{w_1};E_{w_2}\,] \qquad h = f(W_1 x + b_1)$$ $$z = W_2 h + b_2 \qquad q = \text{softmax}(z)$$ $$L = -\ln q_c$$
  • \(x\): the two context-word vectors joined (the input); \(h\): the hidden activation.
  • \(f\): the activation function (like ReLU or tanh). The raw value inside it, \(W_1 x + b_1\), is what we differentiate through.
  • \(z, q\): the output logits and softmax probabilities; \(y\): the one-hot target (\(1\) at the true word \(c\)); \(L = -\ln q_c\): the final loss.

Part 1: Replay one cropped path

This is not a new network. It crops the worksheet's \(D=2, H=2, V=5\) graph down to one visible input, one hidden neuron, and two output words so we can repeat one chain-rule path without carrying every number. We'll set the learning rate \(\eta\) to \(0.1\), assume biases are \(0\), and use the same ReLU activation:

  • Input Data: \(x_1 = 0.5\)
  • Hidden layer weight (W1): \(w_1 = 0.4\) (connects input \(x_1\) to hidden neuron \(h_1\)).
  • Output layer weights (W2): \(w_2 = 0.6\) (connects \(h_1\) to word 1's score \(z_1\)). \(w_3 = -0.5\) (connects \(h_1\) to word 2's score \(z_2\)).
  • The Target: Word 1 is the correct next word (\(y_1 = 1,\ y_2 = 0\)).

1. The Forward Pass
We push the data forward through the pipes to get our prediction:

\(\text{Hidden Input } (net_{h1}) = x_1 \times w_1 = 0.5 \times 0.4 = 0.2\) \(\text{Hidden Output } (h_1) = \text{ReLU}(0.2) = 0.2\) \(\text{Word 1 Logit } (z_1) = h_1 \times w_2 = 0.2 \times 0.6 = 0.12\) \(\text{Word 2 Logit } (z_2) = h_1 \times w_3 = 0.2 \times -0.5 = -0.1\)

Pass logits through Softmax to get probabilities (\(q\)) and Cross-Entropy for Loss (\(L\)):

\(\text{Softmax denominator: } e^{0.12} + e^{-0.1} \approx 2.032\) \(q_1 = 1.127 / 2.032 \approx 0.555 \quad \text{(Model is 55.5\% confident in Word 1)}\) \(q_2 = 0.905 / 2.032 \approx 0.445 \quad \text{(Model is 44.5\% confident in Word 2)}\) \(L = -\ln(q_1) = -\ln(0.555) \approx 0.589\)

2. The Backward Pass (Output Weight \(w_2\))
Our goal is to update \(w_2\) so the loss decreases. The formal mathematical rules for this step dictate exactly what we calculate:

\(1.\ \delta = q - y \qquad\) (Find the Output Blame) \(2.\ \nabla W_2 = \delta h^{\top} \qquad\) (Calculate Output Weight Gradient) \(5.\ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \nabla \theta \qquad\) (Update weight downhill)

The chain rule tells us to multiply the blame at the end by the signal that fed the weight (\(\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_1} \times \frac{\partial z_1}{\partial w_2}\)). Let's execute the rules:

\(\text{Piece 1 (The Blame } \delta_1\text{): } \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_1} = q_1 - y_1 = 0.555 - 1 = -0.445\) \(\text{Piece 2 (The Signal } h_1\text{): } \frac{\partial z_1}{\partial w_2} = h_1 = 0.2\) \(\textbf{Gradient for } \mathbf{w_2} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_2} \right) = -0.445 \times 0.2 = -0.089\) \(\text{Update: } w_2^+ = 0.6 - (0.1 \times -0.089) = 0.6089\)

The weight increased, meaning next time, the network will naturally give a higher score to the correct word.

3. The Backward Pass (Hidden Weight \(w_1\))
This is where it gets interesting. \(w_1\) is buried deep in the network. The formal mathematical rules for passing blame deeper look scary at first glance:

\(3.\ \delta^{(1)} = (W_2^{\top} \delta) \odot f' \qquad\) (Pass Blame down to Hidden Layer) \(4.\ \nabla W_1 = \delta^{(1)} x^{\top} \qquad\) (Calculate Hidden Weight Gradient) \(5.\ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \nabla \theta \qquad\) (Update weight downhill)

That matrix multiplication \((W_2^{\top} \delta)\) is just the formal way to say "sum up the blame from all the paths it connects to". Because the hidden neuron \(h_1\) connects to both \(z_1\) and \(z_2\), it must accept blame from both paths! We must sum them up.

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial h_1} = \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_1} \times \frac{\partial z_1}{\partial h_1} \right) + \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_2} \times \frac{\partial z_2}{\partial h_1} \right)$$

The derivative of a logit with respect to its hidden input is simply its connecting weight (\(w_2\) and \(w_3\)). Let's execute the rules:

\(\text{Piece 1 (Gathering Blame } W_2^{\top}\delta \text{): } \frac{\partial L}{\partial h_1} = (-0.445 \times 0.6) + (0.445 \times -0.5) = -0.4895\) \(\text{Piece 2 (Activation Gate } f' \text{): } \frac{\partial h_1}{\partial net_{h1}} = 1 \quad \text{(Since } 0.2 > 0 \text{, ReLU slope is 1)}\) \(\text{Piece 3 (The Signal } x \text{): } \frac{\partial net_{h1}}{\partial w_1} = x_1 = 0.5\) \(\textbf{Gradient for } \mathbf{w_1} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} \right) = -0.4895 \times 1 \times 0.5 \approx -0.245\) \(\text{Update: } w_1^+ = 0.4 - (0.1 \times -0.245) = 0.4245\)

Part 2: The Macro View (Scaling to Matrices)

The step-by-step math proves there is no magic happening. But an LLM doesn't have 3 weights; it has billions. Calculating that "Gathering Blame" sum (\( \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_1}w_2 + \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_2}w_3 + \dots \)) one-by-one with a `for` loop would take years.

To execute those 5 formal rules instantly, we use Matrices and a secret weapon called the Transpose (\(\top\)). Transposing simply flips a grid of numbers on its side, turning columns into rows.

Let's expand our example to a hidden layer with 3 neurons (\(h\)) and an output layer with 5 vocabulary words (\(z\)). To connect them, we need a \(5 \times 3\) weight matrix (\(W_2\)). In the forward pass, the shapes align perfectly:

$$\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 2 & -1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{W_2 \ (5 \times 3)} \times \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{h \ (3 \times 1)} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 0 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{z \ (5 \times 1)}$$

But when we go backward to "Gather Blame" (Rule 3), the shapes clash. We have 5 pieces of output blame (\(\delta\)) that need to flow back to the 3 hidden neurons. We cannot mathematically multiply a \(5 \times 3\) grid by a column of 5 blames.

By transposing the weights into a \(3 \times 5\) grid (\(W_2^\top\)), we run the plumbing in exact reverse. Notice how the columns of \(W_2\) become the rows of \(W_2^\top\):

$$\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & -1 & 1 \\ 2 & 0 & 1 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{W_2^\top \ (3 \times 5)} \times \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0.1 \\ 0 \\ -0.2 \\ 0 \\ 0.1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\delta \ (5 \times 1)} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0.1 \\ 0.1 \\ 0.1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{hidden blame } (3 \times 1)}$$

Look closely at that matrix multiplication: it is performing the exact "Gathering Blame" sum we did by hand in Step 3, but it does it for all 3 neurons simultaneously!

We use Transpose again to calculate the final weight gradients (Rule 4). To fix the weights, every single one of the 5 output blames must multiply with every single one of the 3 hidden signals. If we stand the 5 blames up in a vertical column and lay the 3 signals flat in a horizontal row (\(h^\top\)), multiplying them draws a full \(5 \times 3\) grid of answers:

$$\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0.1 \\ 0 \\ -0.2 \\ 0 \\ 0.1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\delta \ (5 \times 1)} \times \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{h^\top \ (1 \times 3)} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0.2 & 0.1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -0.4 & -0.2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0.2 & 0.1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{weight updates } (5 \times 3)}$$

This matches the exact shape of our original \(W_2\) matrix, calculating the perfect gradient for all 15 weights in a single mathematical breath.

Click to expand: The Mathematical Proof of the Transpose

The appearance of the transpose (\(\top\)) isn't an arbitrary matrix trick—it is the mathematically unavoidable result of applying the multivariable chain rule to every number in the grid simultaneously.

Let's look at our simple linear layer: \(z = Wx\). In this chapter's output layer, the generic \(M\) below is vocabulary size \(V\), and \(N\) is hidden width \(H\).
Let \(W\) be a matrix of size \(M \times N\).
Let \(x\) be a column vector of size \(N \times 1\).
Let \(z\) be a column vector of size \(M \times 1\).
Let \(\delta = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z}\) be the column vector of blame arriving at \(z\) (size \(M \times 1\)).

Proof 1: The Weight Update (\(\nabla W = \delta x^\top\))

To find how a single specific weight, \(W_{ij}\) (the weight in row \(i\), column \(j\)), affects the final loss \(L\), we use the chain rule. Since weight \(W_{ij}\) only connects to one specific output neuron \(z_i\), the chain rule simplifies to a single path:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_{ij}} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i} \times \frac{\partial z_i}{\partial W_{ij}}$$
  1. We already know \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i} = \delta_i\).
  2. The formula for output neuron \(z_i\) is \(z_i = (W_{i1}x_1) + \dots + (W_{ij}x_j) + \dots\). If we take the derivative of that sum with respect to \(W_{ij}\), everything else drops away, leaving only the input signal \(x_j\). So, \(\frac{\partial z_i}{\partial W_{ij}} = x_j\).

Plugging those in gives us: \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_{ij}} = \delta_i x_j\)

The Matrix Connection: This tells us that the gradient for any weight in the grid is just its corresponding row's blame (\(\delta_i\)) multiplied by its corresponding column's input (\(x_j\)). In linear algebra, the operation that multiplies every element of an \(M \times 1\) column vector (\(\delta\)) by every element of a \(1 \times N\) row vector (\(x^\top\)) to create a full \(M \times N\) grid is called the Outer Product. Therefore, written as full matrices, \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial W} = \delta x^\top\).

Proof 2: Passing Blame (\(\nabla x = W^\top \delta\))

Now we want to find how a single input signal, \(x_j\), affects the final loss \(L\) so we can pass the blame backward to it. Using the multivariable chain rule, we must sum up its effect across all output paths. Unlike a single weight, an input signal \(x_j\) fans out and connects to every single output neuron (\(z_1, \dots, z_M\)).

So, the chain rule requires a sum over all \(M\) outputs:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_j} = \sum_{i=1}^{M} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i} \times \frac{\partial z_i}{\partial x_j} \right)$$
  1. Again, \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i} = \delta_i\).
  2. As before, \(z_i = (W_{i1}x_1) + \dots + (W_{ij}x_j) + \dots\). The derivative of \(z_i\) with respect to \(x_j\) is simply the connecting weight: \(W_{ij}\).

Plugging those in gives us: \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_j} = \sum_{i=1}^{M} \delta_i W_{ij}\)

The Matrix Connection: Look closely at that sum. We are multiplying the elements of a column vector (\(\delta\)) by the elements of a column from our weight matrix (\(W_{1j}, W_{2j}, \dots\)). Standard matrix multiplication requires us to multiply a row by a column. To make the math legal so the computer can calculate the sums for every \(x\) simultaneously, we have to take the \(W\) matrix and lay its columns down horizontally into rows. Flipping columns into rows is exactly what the Transpose (\(W^\top\)) does! Therefore, written as full matrices, \(\frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = W^\top \delta\).

The Golden Rules of Backprop

Because of the magic of matrices, the tedious math we did by hand collapses into the clean formal rules we introduced above. Let \(\delta\) represent the blame arriving at any layer. As we sweep backward, every layer applies these steps:

  • 1. The Weight Update Rule: To find a layer's weight updates, multiply the incoming blame column by the transposed signal row (\(h^\top\) or \(x^\top\)) that fed into it.
  • 2. The Blame-Passing Rule: To pass blame to the layer below, multiply the transposed weights (\(W^\top\)) by the current blame, then multiply by the activation function's local slope (\(f'\)).

A Final Warning

This algorithm—reverse-mode automatic differentiation—is incredibly efficient. However, look closely at Rule 3. Every time we step backward through a layer, we multiply by an activation slope (\(f'\)). If your network is very deep, you are multiplying fraction after fraction. The blame signal gets smaller and smaller until it entirely vanishes before reaching the first layers. This is the infamous vanishing gradient problem, and it is exactly why the modern architectures we cover in Chapter 5 rely heavily on skip connections and normalization to keep the blame flowing clearly.

Production note: why larger models use optimizers such as AdamW

This lab uses the simplest optimizer: subtract the current gradient times one fixed learning rate.

  • Momentum keeps a running direction so noisy updates do not zigzag as much.
  • RMSprop scales each parameter using its recent squared gradients, giving different knobs different effective step sizes.
  • Adam combines momentum-like direction with adaptive scaling.
  • AdamW keeps Adam's adaptive update but separates weight decay, making regularization easier to control.

Those methods change how gradients become steps. They do not replace the forward pass, loss, or backpropagation that calculates the gradients.

Further reading.

If the live examples now feel comfortable, I would go deeper in this order: first the paper behind the little model you just trained, then the classic sources on backpropagation, softmax, and perplexity. You do not need to read them to keep going, but they are the real trailheads.

  1. Ronald T. Kneusel, Math for Deep Learning A practical foundation for this chapter: Chapters 5–6 cover linear algebra and matrix operations, Chapters 7–8 cover differential and matrix calculus, Chapter 10 develops backpropagation, and Chapter 11 covers gradient descent and adaptive optimizers.

  6. Rumelhart, Hinton, and Williams, Learning representations by back-propagating errors The classic backpropagation paper.
  7. Christopher Olah, Calculus on Computational Graphs A visual intuition for backpropagation as local gradients moving through a graph.
